# Situational judgment and personality

**In one line:** in CritiCall3D you also choose the best way to handle difficult call situations, and answer a personality questionnaire built for dispatch work.

These parts belong to CritiCall3D, the newest version. The publisher describes three dimensions. **Operational** is the skills testing covered in the other section guides. **Situational** is a situational judgment test validated for the emergency dispatch environment: you decide the best way to respond to critical incidents, and it includes an emotional intelligence component. **Behavioral** is a multi-factor personality test that measures overall personality plus Dependability, Confident/Assertive, Resilience/Composure and Management/Prioritization. Not every agency uses CritiCall3D, and not every agency that uses it includes all three parts.

## What it measures

- **Judgment under pressure.** Choosing the response that keeps people safe and gets help moving.
- **Emotional control.** Staying calm and respectful with callers who are panicking, rude or abusive.
- **Work style.** The personality part looks at traits linked to dispatch performance, such as dependability and composure.

## How the questions look

**Situational.** A short scenario, such as a screaming caller, an abusive caller or two urgent tasks at once, and several possible responses. You choose the best one, and in some formats also the worst.

**Behavioral.** Statements about yourself that you rate or choose between. There are no right answers in the usual sense. The questionnaire is looking for a pattern that fits the job.

## A method that works

For situational items, run each option through four questions, in this order:

1. **Does it protect life and safety first?** Caller, victim, responders, the public.
2. **Does it get the essential information,** above all the location, as early as possible?
3. **Does it keep control of the call** in a calm, respectful way?
4. **Does it follow procedure** and use the chain of command, rather than improvising or arguing?

The best answer usually passes all four. Options that sound kind but delay help, or sound decisive but skip a step, are the usual traps.

For the personality part:

1. **Answer honestly and consistently.** Questionnaires often ask about the same trait in several ways. Trying to guess the "right" profile tends to produce a contradictory one.
2. **Think about how you act at work,** not at home or on your best day.
3. **Do not overthink single statements.** Your first honest reaction is the most useful answer.

## Traps that cost points

- **Choosing the most sympathetic answer** when it delays the response. Comfort matters, but getting the location and sending help matters more.
- **Matching the caller's emotion.** Raising your voice, arguing back or ending the call on someone who is rude is almost never the best choice.
- **Going it alone.** When a situation is beyond your role, the best answer usually involves your supervisor or the right agency.
- **Promising what you cannot control,** such as "the ambulance will be there in two minutes".
- **Faking the personality test.** Real centers use their own protocols, and the publisher validated this test against job performance, so trying to game it is a poor bet.

## Timing

Situational items usually allow a short read and a decision. Aim for 30 to 45 seconds each. Do not rush the personality questionnaire, but do not dwell either.

## Practice items

These 5 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from CritiCall or any publisher's materials. Real centers follow their own protocols. These answers use the common principles above, not any one agency's rules. Choose the **best** response.

**Item 1.** A caller is screaming and crying so hard you cannot understand what has happened.
A. Tell her to call back when she has calmed down.
B. Speak slowly and firmly, use her name if you have it, and ask one short question: "Where are you?"
C. Raise your voice to match hers so she can hear you.
D. Wait silently until she stops crying.

**Item 2.** A caller says her husband has collapsed but she does not know the address because they are visiting friends.
A. Tell her you cannot send help without an address.
B. Send help to her home address instead.
C. Ask her to drive him to the hospital herself.
D. Ask her to look for mail, a street sign or a neighbor, or to ask the friends, while you check any location your system shows.

**Item 3.** A caller reports a minor fender-bender with no injuries. While you are taking details, a second line rings and the screen shows it as a possible cardiac arrest.
A. Finish the fender-bender call fully first, since it came in first.
B. Get the essential details from the first caller quickly, put them on hold or end the call safely as procedure allows, and take the cardiac call.
C. Ignore the second line and let someone else get it.
D. Hang up on the first caller without a word.

**Item 4.** A caller who rings several times a week with non-emergency complaints calls again, saying he smells smoke.
A. Tell him to stop calling 911 about nothing.
B. Log it as a nuisance call.
C. Ask him to call the non-emergency line.
D. Treat this call on its own facts and process it as a possible fire.

**Item 5.** A colleague on the next console is overwhelmed and has missed a radio call from a unit.
A. Ignore it, since it is not your radio.
B. Answer the unit yourself, without telling anyone.
C. Let your colleague or supervisor know at once, and help if procedure allows.
D. Report your colleague later for missing the call.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 5</summary>

**Item 1: B.** A firm, slow voice and one simple question helps a panicking caller focus, and location comes first. *Why not D:* waiting wastes time that may matter. *Why not A:* she may not be able to call back.

**Item 2: D.** Getting a location is the priority, and there are many ways to find one. *Why not A:* refusing help is never the best answer. *Why not B:* that sends help to the wrong place.

**Item 3: B.** The possible cardiac arrest is a threat to life. Closing the first call safely and quickly, then taking the second, follows the life-first logic. *Why not D:* dropping the first caller without a word is poor practice and may lose information you need.

**Item 4: D.** Each call is judged on its own facts. Smoke can mean fire. *Why not A, B or C:* a frequent caller can still have a real emergency.

**Item 5: C.** A missed radio call can put a crew at risk. Telling your colleague or supervisor at once gets it covered properly. *Why not B:* acting silently can create confusion about who is handling it.
