# Decision making and prioritization

**In one line:** a short emergency message flashes up, often while you are busy with something else, and you have a few seconds to send the right agency.

This is the heart of CritiCall. The publisher lists it as Multi-Tasking / Advanced Decision Making: you make decisions under a short time limit while doing another task at the same time. In the newer CritiCall3D, the Operational part folds this into a single combined test.

## What it measures

Three things at once:

- **Rule following.** You get a fixed set of rules and must apply them exactly, even when your gut says something else.
- **Speed.** The candidate preparation guide says you have 15 seconds to answer each emergency message. A wrong agency or a late answer gets no credit.
- **Divided attention.** The messages can pop up while you are typing or answering other questions. You have to switch, decide, and switch back.

Many agencies also look at **prioritization**: which call matters most when several arrive at once. CritiCall lists prioritization among the skills its tests need, and the Behavioral part of CritiCall3D reports a Management/Prioritization factor.

## How the questions look

A one-line message appears on screen, something like "Car on fire in a parking lot". Four symbols sit below it: Police, Fire, EMS (emergency medical service) and Utility. You click one. Sometimes the message arrives on its own. Sometimes it interrupts another task, such as entering caller details.

### The four rules

The candidate guide asks you to learn four decision rules before test day and to use only those rules, not what you know about any real agency. In our own words:

| Send | When the message describes |
|---|---|
| **Police** | Someone trying or threatening to hurt a person, someone who has already hurt a person, or someone damaging (or in the middle of damaging) another person's property |
| **Fire** | Visible signs of a fire in progress, such as flames or smoke; a fire alarm going off; or a person who is trapped or shut in and needs to be rescued or freed |
| **EMS** | An emergency medical condition that needs medically trained people |
| **Utility** | A broken or malfunctioning public water system, electric power system (including power lines, streetlights and traffic signals), natural gas system used for home heating, or a blocked sewer drain |

Read the [official CritiCall Candidate Test Preparation Guide](https://criticall911.com/hubfs/Criticall-Website-Files/CritiCall_Candidate_Test_Preparation_Guide_2023.pdf) for the exact wording. It is short, free and worth reading twice.

## A method that works

1. **Learn the four rules until you can say them without looking.** The guide says applicants who know the rules well do better on these items. Make flashcards: trigger words on one side, agency on the other.
2. **Find the trigger, not the story.** Every message has one fact that matches a rule: "smoke", "trapped", "not breathing", "punching", "power line down". Ignore the rest.
3. **Ask the rules in a fixed order** if you are unsure: Is a person being harmed or property damaged? (Police.) Fire, smoke, alarm or someone trapped? (Fire.) A medical emergency? (EMS.) Broken water, power, gas or sewer? (Utility.)
4. **Answer, then go straight back** to whatever task the message interrupted. Do not reread a message you have already answered.
5. **For prioritization, rank by danger to life right now.** A life at risk this minute comes first. Next comes a threat that is still unfolding. Property crimes that are already over come last. Real centers follow their own written protocols, so treat this as the common logic, not a universal rule.

## Traps that cost points

- **Reaching for EMS whenever someone might be hurt.** A person trapped in a lift or a locked car goes to **Fire** under the rules, because they need to be freed. A person being attacked goes to **Police**, because someone is harming them.
- **Using real-world knowledge.** In real life a gas leak might bring the fire service. On this test, a broken home-heating gas system is **Utility** if nothing is burning. Follow the rules as written.
- **Missing the alarm rule.** A fire alarm that is sounding is **Fire**, even with no smoke.
- **Treating property damage as Utility.** A vandal smashing a streetlight is damaging property, so **Police**. A streetlight that is simply out is **Utility**.
- **Running out the clock.** No answer after 15 seconds scores the same as a wrong one. If you are torn, pick the best fit and move on.
- **Skipping.** The guide warns that you are penalized for skipped questions unless the instructions say otherwise.

## Timing

Fifteen seconds per message is enough if you know the rules, and not nearly enough if you have to think them through. Aim to answer in five seconds or less in practice, so you have room to spare when the message interrupts typing. Prioritization questions are not on a 15-second clock in every format, but treat them as quick decisions too.

## Practice items

These 5 items are original, written for these notes. They are not taken from CritiCall or any publisher's materials. For items 1 to 4, choose **Police, Fire, EMS or Utility** using only the four rules above. Give yourself 10 seconds each.

**Item 1.** The traffic signal at a busy junction is stuck on red in every direction.

**Item 2.** A toddler is locked inside a car in a hot parking lot and his mother cannot open the door.

**Item 3.** A man in a parking lot is swinging a baseball bat at people walking past.

**Item 4.** The fire alarm is ringing throughout an apartment building. No one can see smoke.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, items 1 to 4</summary>

**Item 1: Utility.** Traffic signals are named in the Utility rule as part of the electric power system. *Why not Police:* nobody is causing harm or damage. The signal has simply malfunctioned.

**Item 2: Fire.** The child is trapped in a car and needs to be released, which is the Fire rule. *Why not EMS:* the message does not describe a medical condition yet. The urgent need is getting him out, and the rules give that job to Fire.

**Item 3: Police.** He is trying to physically harm other people. *Why not EMS:* the message does not say anyone has been injured, and even if they had, the person attacking is the trigger for Police.

**Item 4: Fire.** A sounding fire alarm is its own trigger under the Fire rule. *Why not Police:* a false alarm might turn out to be a prank, but the message does not say anyone pulled it, and the rule does not require smoke.

</details>

For item 5, choose which call should be handled first. Use the common rule: an immediate threat to life first.

**Item 5.** Three calls are waiting.
A. A store reports a shoplifter who left 20 minutes ago.
B. A caller says a man is standing on the outside of a bridge railing, saying he is going to jump.
C. A driver reports a dog wandering along the shoulder of a quiet road.

<details>
<summary>Answers and explanations, item 5</summary>

**Item 5: B.** A person is threatening to end their life right now, which is the most urgent threat to life. *Why not C:* a loose dog can cause a crash, so it matters, but the risk is lower and less immediate. *Why not A:* the theft is over and the suspect is gone.

</details>

## Score yourself

| Item | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Answer | Utility | Fire | Police | Fire | B |

- **5:** you know the rules. Now practice them while doing something else, such as typing a paragraph and having a friend read you messages.
- **3 to 4:** look at which rule you broke. Most misses come from items 2 and 4, where common sense points somewhere other than the rule.
- **2 or fewer:** go back to the rules table and drill flashcards until you answer each one in under five seconds.
